# X Regulation or Statute Explainer Threads

> A long thread that walks one bill, statute, rule, or ordinance in its own order and answers the public objections where the text answers them. For short narrative threads, see [x-posts.md](x-posts.md#threading-best-practices). For voice and quality tests, see [x-writing-craft.md](x-writing-craft.md).

## When to use this format

Use it when all three hold:

1. There's **one governing text** the reader can open: a filed bill, enrolled act, agency rule, ordinance, or permit order.
2. The topic is **contested**, and people are repeating claims about the text that the text itself settles.
3. The writer can **cite section numbers**. If you can't point to the section, write commentary instead.

Don't use it for news of a vote, a hot take, a trend, or a study. Those are short narrative threads (x-posts.md) or X Articles (x-articles.md). If the reader can't read the source text, the core move of this format falls flat.

## Why it works

The format earns trust from skeptics. The bill sets the order of the thread, so the reader can see nothing was skipped and follow along in the PDF. Objections get answered where the provision sits, so every rebuttal carries its own citation. That produces what X rewards for reference material: bookmarks and quotes. People save it to check later and quote it as ammunition in their own fights.

**Exemplar.** @jamesdickey on Texas SB 2 (2025 school choice, as filed), 19 parts: https://x.com/jamesdickey/status/1883698126898532405. Hook: 13.2k impressions, 62 likes, 26 reposts, 11 quotes, **24 bookmarks**. That's about 4x the most-bookmarked post in a later 30-day sample of the same account (max 6 across 62 posts, August 2026). About 56% of readers who opened part 2 reached part 19. The closing "read it yourself" post had the second-highest like count in the thread. One thread is a sample of one, posted during the peak of the session fight. Treat the numbers as a floor to beat.

## Where this overrides the narrative-thread rules

x-posts.md's threading rules target 5 to 7 tweet narrative arcs. An explainer breaks four of them on purpose:

| Narrative-thread rule | Explainer rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 5 to 7 tweets | As many as the text has contested sections (often 10 to 20) | The length *is* the proof of completeness. Shrink uncontested sections to one line. Don't drop them. |
| Under 280 chars per tweet | Long posts throughout, including part 1 (needs Premium) | A provision plus its objection plus the answer rarely fits in 280, and the myth-list hook runs 500 to 700 characters. Put the title and the first 2 or 3 objections above the "Show more" fold, so the preview still stops the scroll. Without Premium, use an X Article instead (x-articles.md). |
| End on a debatable question | End on "read it yourself" | The closer hands over the primary source. That shows confidence, and debate happens in the replies anyway. |
| Links go in a self-reply | The source link goes in the closing post | The link is the payoff of the close. By the last post, the reader has already given the thread their time, so the reach penalty costs little. |

Everything else in x-posts.md still applies: no link in the hook, soft transitions, the reply-back window, and fresh closers. The writing skill's universal rules also still apply. Two need care in this format:

- **Rule #10 (no corrective reframing).** An objection plus a verdict is a rebuttal, and it's allowed. Follow the verdict with the provision or the arithmetic, never with a restated label ("That's not a ban. It's a building code."). Vary the verdicts, too. Five "No." posts in a row read as a formula.
- **Rule #21 (open a decision piece on the decision).** This format deliberately cites section numbers and enabling authority, because the reader is checking the text. Keep instrument history (earlier drafts, prior versions) out of the thread anyway.

## The skeleton

**Part 1: myth-list hook.** This is the only part most people see (about 90% never open part 2), so it has to work alone.

```
[Topic]: Fact vs. Fiction

[Objection, in the opponent's words]!
[Objection from the other flank]!
[Objection]!
... (6 to 8 total, one per line)

Think of [the thing] as [plain analogy]:
 - [what it is, 1]
 - [what it is, 2]
 - [what it is, 3]

But the details matter a LOT, and they address the concerns above.

So let's look at [bill/rule number] as [filed/enacted/adopted] and see what it actually contains:

1/N
```

- Write each objection **the way opponents actually say it**, exclamation marks included. Don't soften them into strawmen. A reader should recognize their own fear in the first screen.
- Pull objections from **both flanks** when they exist ("It'll kill public schools!" next to "It'll kill private schools!"). This is what gets the thread past people who already agree.
- Name the **version** ("as filed," "as enrolled," "adopted rule"). Bills change. Naming the version protects the thread later.

**Part 2: purpose or authority.** Quote the purpose clause or the enabling authority (constitutional article, statute the rule implements). Tie it to what the reader already respects. Close with a turn into the walk ("Now let's get into the details!").

**Parts 3 to N-1: the statute walk.** One section (or a tight cluster) per post, in the text's own order.

```
Sec. [number] [does/sets/requires] [plain summary].

[Only if a myth lives here:]
"[The objection, quoted]"
[Verdict: No. / Yes. / Not in the text.] [The provision, arithmetic, or redefinition that answers it.]

n/N
```

- **Open every post with the section number.** It's the citation, and it's a landmark for readers following along.
- **Concede before you reframe.** "Yes. That's the max." earns more trust than denying a true number.
- **Do the worst-case math.** Take the scariest number, divide it per person, and put it next to a figure the reader already knows (the local district's per-student spend, the city's budget line). Link the benchmark's source.
- **Define the loaded word with one everyday analogy.** "Strings are unrelated requirements, like gas money only if you stop by the store for me." Then state the provision plainly and let the reader draw the conclusion. Once per thread is enough.
- **Group boring sections, but only neighbors.** Adjacent low-stakes sections (enforcement next to severability, severability next to effective date) can share one post. Don't pull a later section forward to fill a post. That breaks the text's order, and readers following along in the PDF lose their place. Don't skip boring sections either. Leaving them out breaks the completeness promise.

**Part N: verify-it-yourself close.**

```
Don't take my word for any of the above. Read the [bill/rule] yourself at [link]. [One line on how readable it is: page count, plain language.]

N/N
```

This is the one post where a link belongs. It goes last, after the reader has already given the thread their time.

**After posting: stay in the replies.**
- Self-reply with the ask: "Help others learn about it. Like and share the first post in this thread." Also tag the sponsor and allied orgs here, not in the hook.
- Answer pushback with the **words the thread already defined** ("guardrail"). Readers have already accepted those terms.
- Invite correction with a stake: "Point out any mistake I made in the N posts above. Follow the link to the text. I'll wait." Only write this if every claim really is pinned to a section.

## Production rules

- **Write the whole thread before posting part 1**, then post all parts within 20 to 30 minutes. A thread that trickles out over an hour loses readers between parts.
- **Draft variations of part 1 only.** Where write-x asks for 3+ variations, vary the hook (objection picks, order, analogy). Write the walk once. The text fixes its order.
- **Number every part n/N**, including the purpose post. A visible total is a finish line, and it tells skeptics you aren't hiding a section.
- **Every factual claim about the text cites a section.** Claims about effects ("proven to improve outcomes," "other states saw more private schools") need an outside source in the post or in a reply. Otherwise cut them. They're the weakest links, and critics will pull on them.
- **Timing:** post within days of the text's filing or publication, while the argument is live. The exemplar went out in the first weeks of the session, while the bill was the top fight.
- **Voice:** first person is fine in the purpose post, the closing post, and the replies ("I'd oppose any requirement the text doesn't contain"). The walk itself stays in third person about the text. This overrides x-writing-craft.md's "avoid I in body tweets" only where the writer is taking a stated position.

## Pre-publish checklist (explainer-specific)

Run this after the writing skill's universal self-audit and checklist.

- [ ] Part 1 works as a standalone post with no link, names the text's version, and puts the title plus 2 or 3 objections above the fold.
- [ ] 6 to 8 objections, phrased as opponents say them, from more than one side where more than one side exists.
- [ ] Every body post opens with a section number, in the text's own order. Grouped sections are adjacent.
- [ ] Every myth from part 1 is answered somewhere in the walk. Tick each one off.
- [ ] No verdict is followed by a restated label (rule #10). Verdicts vary.
- [ ] No effects claim without a source.
- [ ] Every number that's the writer's own arithmetic shows its inputs.
- [ ] Closing post links the primary source on the legislature's or agency's own site.
- [ ] Self-reply with the share ask is drafted and ready.
